Motion Granted

A test, not an indicator

What it means

At least one judge had a question the written briefs didn't fully answer. Not surprising in a case this complex with this many issues and this many briefing gaps. But all three concurred and none pushed back on granting.

What it doesn't mean

Courts grant argument in cases they affirm all the time. It is not a signal of reversal, it's a pressure test for both sides. The defense gets its opportunity and the State will be under a microscope for every briefing gap they left open.

Issue I: The Franks Challenge

The core question

Was the probable cause affidavit (PCA) for Allen's home fundamentally misleading? Under Franks v. Delaware: if the warrant was secured through deliberate falsehood or reckless disregard for the truth, the evidence it produced falls, including the gun.

The fruit question

If the warrant falls, the physical evidence falls. Fruit of the poisonous tree. This is why Issue I matters structurally even when it carries the steepest hill label.

Steepest hill Altice will be the most skeptical. Vaidik and Brown will be more open.
Issue I

Why Issue I Is the Hardest Sell for Altice

He wrote affidavits

As a deputy prosecutor and Chief of the Felony Division in Marion County, Altice drafted and supervised probable cause affidavits. He knows what goes in, what gets left out, and where the line between advocacy and misrepresentation sits in practice. A Franks challenge accuses that person of deliberate dishonesty. That accusation lands differently on a judge who spent years being the person on the other side.

He evaluated them from the bench

Marion Superior Court Criminal Division 2, 2001 to 2012: 250 major felony jury trials, 75 murder trials, 7 death penalty cases. He spent a decade deciding whether affidavits were sufficient. His internal standard for what constitutes a defective affidavit was formed by seeing hundreds of them. That instinct doesn't disappear when you move to the appellate bench.

The ceiling

Being the steepest hill doesn't make him a wall. Ramos-Osario (April 2025): Altice authored the Fourth Amendment reversal when the State's evidentiary foundation was objectively insufficient. He reversed and Vaidik concurred. He will reverse on Fourth Amendment grounds when the State simply hasn't done the work. The question for Issue I: are the PCA defects that clear-cut and objective?

Issue II: The Involuntary Confession Argument

The core question

Were Allen's jail confessions the product of psychotic deterioration under extended solitary confinement at Westville? The defense argues he was not mentally capable of voluntary waiver by the time he made those statements. The IPAS Settlement, a federal court settlement governing mentally ill prisoners in IDOC, is central to the conditions argument.

Why the confessions impeach themselves

Allen confessed to things family members denied at trial. In one case, he could not remember confessing days later. The defense argues the confessions don't just raise voluntariness questions, they raise questions about what was actually said and remembered. The State's counter is Connelly: coercion requires intentional state action, not just adverse conditions. That argument is most likely to resonate with Altice.

Strongest path
Issue II

Why Brown Is the Most Receptive on Issue II

The drug court she built

Brown didn't just advocate for drug courts, she created Dubois County's drug court, its Community Corrections Advisory Board, and the Substance Abuse Council. She has spent her career building structures premised on the belief that mental illness interacts with the justice system in ways the system isn't equipped to handle fairly. Allen's confession argument is premised on exactly that reasoning.

The Tillett dissent

Tillett v. State (March 2025): Brown reversed a conviction to protect a defendant with schizophrenia spectrum disorder's right to present an insanity defense. Altice dissented. The Indiana Supreme Court later reversed Brown. At the COA level, where Allen's case is decided first, her threshold for taking psychotic deterioration seriously as a legal claim is lower than the Supreme Court's. That's the level that matters here.

Her own words

"Getting to make decisions that are fair and in accordance with the law is most rewarding. On the opposite end are the decisions that don't seem fair but nonetheless are what the law demands." That tension is precisely what Issue II asks the panel to sit with. A man smearing feces, drinking toilet water, confessing to things his family denied. Fair and lawful is the question.

Issue III: The Right to Present a Complete Defense

Thread A: Third-party culpability

The Odinism/ritualistic theory the trial court largely excluded. The defense wasn't floating speculation. They were arguing a specific alternative explanation for the physical evidence the jury had a right to evaluate for themselves as the fact-finders. Doctrine: Chambers v. Mississippi, Holmes v. South Carolina, Lashbrook v. State.

Thread B: The Napue duty

Did the prosecution allow false or misleading testimony to stand uncorrected? The Weber van. The Holeman testimony. Glossip v. Oklahoma (2025), one of the State's four briefing gaps, reaffirmed and sharpened the Napue doctrine after the State filed its response. They have no written position on what it means for this case.

Strong path
Issue III

The Passarelli Signal: Brown vs. Altice on the Record

Altice: Passarelli Majority

Authored the opinion affirming exclusion of a murder defendant's PTSD expert testimony. Reasoning: the legal test uses an objective "reasonable person" standard, so testimony about the defendant's individual mental state doesn't change the applicable standard and doesn't belong before the jury. Deference to the trial court's evidentiary call.

Brown: Passarelli Dissent

Dissented. Position: PTSD colors a person's perception of danger in ways genuinely relevant to what the jury needs to understand. The testimony should have been admitted. She broke from Altice specifically on whether this murder defendant had the right to put this evidence before the jury. The fault line in Passarelli is the same fault line in Allen's Issue III.

Vaidik is the deciding factor and her record suggests she sides closer to Brown's instinct on a defendant's right to present a defense than to Altice's instinct on deference to the trial court.

Issue IV: The Sleeper Issue

What the doctrine says

Indiana courts will reverse when the combined weight of trial errors, taken together, denied the defendant a fundamentally fair trial, even if no individual error would have reversed alone. The question stops being "was this one ruling reversible error?" It becomes: was this a fair trial?

Why it's the sleeper issue

Some may dismiss this as the backup argument. It isn't. It operates at a different level. It doesn't need to win any single argument, it needs to win the math. The near-misses on Issues I, II, and III don't stay close calls in isolation, they stack. The standard becomes: was this a fair trial, or not?

The unanswered argument
Issue IV
The State's Silence

On paper, an unanswered argument is still heard. The harm is quiet but real.

'Counsel, the appellant raised cumulative error in their opening brief. You didn't address it in your response. Why not? Do you concede the doctrine applies?'

At oral argument, quiet harm becomes loud.

Vaidik knows what a briefing gap looks like. Brown has shown she finds for defendants when the equities are bad enough. Altice, as a former prosecutor, knows you don't leave arguments unanswered. Unanswered arguments look like concessions.

The four arguments the State left unanswered through three rounds of briefing

01  ·  Glossip v. Oklahoma (2025)

SCOTUS reaffirmed and sharpened the Napue doctrine after the State filed its response. Allen's reply raises it directly. The State walks into Sept 21 with a controlling Supreme Court decision on one of Rick's arguments and no written position on it.

02  ·  Cumulative Error

Three rounds of briefing. The State never engaged with the doctrine. They didn't argue it doesn't apply, didn't dispute the errors add up. They said nothing. At oral argument, that is an open target in front of three judges.

03  ·  Issue II.D: The Fruit Test

If the confession is involuntary, does that also suppress everything police found after Allen confessed? The State chose not to answer. Their written defense of that downstream evidence is blank.

04  ·  The ISP's 2019 Reversal

Internal ISP documents cast doubt on how the investigation characterized the Blair/Carbaugh statements. The State's brief doesn't engage with their own agency's paper trail. The silence is more notable because of it.
