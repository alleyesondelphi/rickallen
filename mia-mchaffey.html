Confessions

Mia McHaffey (Juror #02 at trial, Voir Dire #08): The Delphi Murders: First Person Podcast (Jan. 2025)

Audio interview with hosts Aine and Kevin (Murder Sheet). Mia was identified anonymously as "Juror #08" (her voir dire number). She was one of the initial undecided voters and ultimately voted guilty.

Assertions & Reasoning

Deliberation process

First vote (day two of deliberations): 9 guilty, 3 undecided. After rewatching interview and bridge videos on Saturday: 8 guilty, 4 undecided. Final Monday vote: unanimous guilty. The three-day shift was driven by weekend reflection and Monday open discussion between undecided and decided jurors.

Timeline

"What we got to first" was establishing that Richard Allen was Bridge Guy. The jury built a physical timeline using sticky notes on the large map exhibit, placing phone activity, sightings, and clothing descriptions in order. Once the clothing self-identification was confirmed, the juror said "you really can't" dispute that Richard Allen is Bridge Guy. From there, she found it "pretty easy to add all of those things together."

Confessions

The van mention was the standout from the confession evidence. "Looking back, I think, like, who would confess that many times that doesn't actually do that? I can't think of how that would ever happen." The juror gave minimal weight to other confession details, but the van reference stood out as specific and unprompted.

Bullet / Oberg testimony

This juror gave no weight to the bullet evidence in reaching her verdict. "I kind of threw out...the whole science of the bullet. It wasn't Oberg as a person at all. I think she was so professional...She believes in her clients." The skepticism arose from the defense's cross-examination and the testimony of defense expert Warren, who evaluated only photographs rather than the physical evidence. "I just can't say that I believe for sure that the evidence is exact."

Interrogation

On the Holeman interview: found it "overdone" and felt Allen's response was consistent with someone who genuinely didn't know how a bullet could have ended up at the scene. "If I was being asked those questions and I didn't know how that got there, how am I going to explain how it did if I don't know?" She viewed Allen as "just too calm" and appearing like he "really didn't know anything to have done" it. The interrogation video caused the vote to dip from 9–3 to 8-4 after Saturday viewing.

Sarah Carbaugh

This juror said the group decided "as a whole" to "throw out" Sarah Carbaugh's testimony. "Not that she's an unlike[able], she's a hard to believe person. But I think the defense did a good job trying to discount her." The juror applied the standard: "If we didn't believe that, would that change the outcome?" For Carbaugh, the answer was no, so the testimony was set aside.

Westville prison videos

"Going into it, it wasn't as bad as I expected." The juror had anticipated much more severe footage. Allen appeared "black and blue" in one segment where he had hit his head. In cell videos, a "Little Max" (assistant Max Baker) mixed up which segments showed Allen clothed vs. unclothed, causing some jurors to see content out of order.

Deliberation fairness

One juror raised the question: "If it wasn't Richard Allen, then who could it have possibly been?" This juror pushed back on that framing. "That seems like a wrong question to ask because that's not what this is about. We're not seeing if it could be anyone else. Is there enough evidence showing that it's him specifically?" She tried to keep deliberations focused on the standard of proof, not alternative suspects.

Prosecution shortcomings

A jury question about how many cars matched Allen's vehicle model existed, and the prosecution had to look it up on the spot. The juror was critical: "I can't believe you wouldn't have thought to do that already." She also noted recurring technology failures with the courtroom monitor as a source of frustration, citing the waste of sequestration time.

Defense attorneys

Brad Rosie came across as "intimidating" and his questioning felt like "smoke and mirrors." Defense preparation generally appeared "scrambled, confused, not knowing what they're going to do next." Andrew Baldwin was viewed more favorably. Jennifer Ojeda was a standout positive: "She has a little bit of attitude. And I feel like that, from what I would picture like a defense attorney to be, I just, I liked it."

Internal Inconsistencies

1 Discarded bullet evidence but analyzed it in exhaustive detail
Claims to have set it aside
"I kind of threw out the whole science of the bullet... in making my decision, I didn't consider it at all."
Engaged with it substantively
She evaluated Oberg's methodology, compared it to Warren's photo-only approach, concluded the defense expert's method was inferior ("you're definitely not supposed to do that"), and formed a specific view on why the science was uncertain. The level of engagement goes well beyond something she "threw out."

Disregarding evidence and concluding it is scientifically unreliable are two different things. Her reasoning process shows she did consider the bullet; she simply concluded the science was too uncertain to rely on. That is a substantive finding, not a non-consideration.

2 "Timeline more important than confessions" vs. the van confession stood out
Downplays confessions as a category
"Things that he reported himself is really what got me closest." Emphasizes the timeline and clothing self-description over the confession record. Host Aine also confirms: "the timeline was perhaps more important than any of this sort of flashier confessions."
The van was the one exception she couldn't dismiss
"Of course, what stands out to everyone is the van." She singles out the van mention as "one of the things that I had that stood out to me in a sense of thinking that he could be guilty." She is unable to hold the position that confessions were irrelevant while granting the van reference its own category of weight.

She claims the timeline drove her verdict but cannot cleanly separate that from the van confession, which she acknowledges carried independent weight. The two framings of her decision-making sit in tension.

3 Claims rigorous devil's advocate approach while describing emotional collapse
Presents herself as a skeptical, deliberate reasoner
"I really tried to play devil's advocate and just see it from the defense's perspective...I did try to question everything. I just, it felt like a really hard thing to do. And I just didn't want to get it wrong, no matter what."
Also describes being emotionally overwhelmed
"Towards the end, I was kind of a mess." "I was just, like, I already knew, like, I was going to be done. And just, it was such an overwhelming feeling." "I would say out of everyone, I definitely had the worst time." She describes crying when the unanimous vote was read.

She presents her process as methodical skepticism but simultaneously describes a level of emotional distress that raises questions about whether the devil's advocate framing reflects how she actually experienced deliberations, or how she prefers to describe them in retrospect.

4 Interrogation video looked favorable to Allen, but she voted guilty
Found the Holeman interrogation sympathetic to Allen

She found his demeanor consistent with innocence.
The video shifted votes toward undecided, then she voted guilty anyway
After the Saturday rewatch, the count moved from 9-3 (guilty/undecided) to 8-4. The video evidently moved at least one guilty vote to undecided. Yet Mia, who described the interrogation most sympathetically, ultimately voted guilty. She does not explain how she reconciled her reading of the interrogation with her final verdict.

Her stated interpretation of the Holeman video would logically support an undecided or not guilty position. She offers no account of what overrode that reading.
